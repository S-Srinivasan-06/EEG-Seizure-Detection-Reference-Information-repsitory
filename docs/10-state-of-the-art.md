# 10. State of the art by task category

[Contents](../README.md) | [Chapter 9: Agreement and interpretation](09-agreement-and-interpretation.md) | [Next: Research gaps](11-research-gaps.md)

There is no defensible single “best seizure detector” across these studies. The categories below differ in modality, population, evaluation unit, and clinical role. Here, “state of the art” means the most informative evidence class identified in this review, not an overall winner.

| Category | Strongest evidence in this inventory | Defensible claim | Still unestablished |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short-window or clip classification | DGDCN on TUSZ 12- and 60-second segments; CBraMod on CHB-MIT 10-second clips; LaBraM/REVE on abnormality or event-type clips | Models classify labeled windows under their stated dataset and split. DGDCN reports AUC 88.7%/90.4%; CBraMod reports balanced accuracy 0.7398, AUPRC 0.3689, and AUROC 0.8892 [V]. | Event detection, false alarms/day, continuous deployment, or direct comparisons across different labels and splits. LaBraM’s TUEV/TUAB tasks and REVE’s enumerated tasks are not verified continuous seizure protocols. |
| Patient-independent/cross-dataset scalp event detection | Peh et al.’s six-center, five-independent-dataset event assessment ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1142/S0129065723500120)); Yang’s large frozen external adult inference | External testing and event-based scoring exist. Yang reports 76.68% sensitivity and about 56 false alarms/day on 1,006 RPAH sessions [V]. | A universally acceptable alarm operating point, low-burden performance across sites/devices, and prospective routine-service benefit. Keep Peh cohort-specific values separate and verify the primary table before quoting exact values. |
| Prospective scalp EMU device performance | Fürbass’s multicenter prospective evaluation; later retrospective head-to-head studies by Reus and Koren | Bounded prospective performance exists: Fürbass reports 81% sensitivity (95% CI 74-86%) and 7.1 false alarms/day for unequivocal seizures [V]. Retrospective comparisons quantify operating-point trade-offs. | Randomized evidence that adult EMU algorithms reduce response time, staff hours, injuries, or cost. Rommens is retrospective despite “online” in the title. |
| Adult ICU/inpatient continuous EEG | Ganguly’s large record-level service cohort plus a small event-level pilot; quantitative EEG (qEEG)-assisted reader workflow studies | Real-world record-level Persyst sensitivity/PPV, event-level pilot estimates, and human review time/accuracy trade-offs have been reported. | Record PPV 0.08 cannot be treated as event precision. Broad external validation, prospective alarm-response effects, and patient-outcome benefit remain unestablished. |
| Neonatal detection and assistance | Hogan et al.’s held-out multi-reviewer validation; ANSeR randomized multicenter trial | Hogan reports time-sample sensitivity and false event detections on held-out neonatal cohorts. ANSeR improved seizure-hour recognition by 20.8 points (95% CI 3.6-37.1), while individual-neonate sensitivity did not improve and inappropriate medication use did not differ significantly [V]. | Hogan is retrospective and has no bedside utility or onset-latency result. ANSeR did not establish long-term outcome benefit. Neither finding transfers automatically to older populations. |
| Wearable and ambulatory review | Hartmann’s two-channel scalp-derived ambulatory workflow; SeizeIT2 behind-ear EEG/ECG validation; retrospective detector work on actual subscalp EEG | Long-duration candidate triage is feasible; expert review can trade sensitivity for precision. SeizeIT2’s offline algorithm-alone and review-assisted results are distinct endpoints. | Free-living prospective effectiveness, acceptable end-user alarm burden, and measured long-term workload/outcome savings. The 2026 SeizeIT2 analysis was offline despite prospective cohort collection. |
| Subscalp acquisition | UMPIRE prospective bilateral subscalp feasibility study | Acquisition and clinical signal yield were feasible in the studied cohort. | Independent full-record detector accuracy. Candidate-conditioned review prevents estimating sensitivity and specificity. |
| Intracranial SEEG/iEEG | PPi seizure-specific patient-group pretraining; long-term iEEG CNN/transformer benchmarks | Seizure-specific representation learning and continuous intracranial event detection are active, modality-specific areas. | Transfer to scalp EEG, broad prospective multi-hospital validation, and general detector performance independent of patient-specific programming. |
| Foundation models | LaBraM, CBraMod, REVE, and PPi address different pretraining/downstream questions | Generic pretraining can be assessed on downstream tasks. CBraMod has an explicit CHB-MIT clip endpoint and a fine-tuned LaBraM clip baseline; PPi has an explicit SEEG seizure endpoint. | Foundation-model superiority for continuous seizure alarms. LaBraM’s original paper reports abnormality/event-type tasks; REVE’s seizure-specific protocol was not identified; CBraMod remains clip-level without event alarms. |
| Reader assistance and workflow | Yang’s 66-session adult pilot; ANSeR’s randomized seizure-hour outcome; qEEG/CSA review studies | Some human-in-the-loop or visualization workflows improve a measured intermediate endpoint or reduce review time in a bounded study. Yang reports 90 versus 7.62 minutes in its separate pilot [V]; ANSeR improves seizure-hour recognition. | Generalizable staffing/cost reduction or patient benefit. Data reduction, retrospective earlier flags, and reviewer-time estimates do not equal sustained service time saved. |

## Overall assessment

The field has moved beyond proof-of-concept clips: cross-dataset event evaluation, prospective EMU device performance, randomized neonatal assistance, adult external inference, and small reader-workflow studies all exist. Each supports a bounded claim. The evidence does not identify one universal winner or show that foundation models, dynamic graphs, or a single sensitivity score solve continuous detection across EMU, ICU, neonatal, ambulatory, wearable, and intracranial settings.

A credible state-of-the-art claim should name the category, frozen or adapted protocol, reference definition, event/record/patient unit, alarm operating point, continuous negative exposure, and whether evaluation was retrospective, prospective, or randomized.

## Cross-cutting frontier evidence

| Area | Strongest candidate evidence | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Common benchmark | Revised SzCORE challenge: private continuous scalp test set, patient-held-out evaluation across 28 architectures, standardized scoring. The non-peer-reviewed version 2 preprint reports best F1 0.32, sensitivity 0.37, and precision 0.29 [V] ([version 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18191v2)). | The most useful common challenge-style evidence identified here, but not a definitive leaderboard: the test set is private and results are challenge-specific. |
| Open and reproducible resources | Public SzCORE framework; author repository for PPi; DGDCN repository with static-audit concerns; CBraMod task appendices and split descriptions. | Openness does not establish reproduction. Versioned data/manifests, runnable pinned code, and independent reruns are needed before treating results as reproduced. |
| Real-time and edge deployment | Prospective online EMU device work such as Fürbass shows that online performance can be measured; patient-specific low-power iEEG work such as Laelaps addresses constrained hardware. | No cross-setting edge leader is established. DGDCN’s posted attention/convolution branch is noncausal, and no latency or compute benchmark for its full stream pipeline was reported. |
| Emerging architectures | Larger neonatal ConvNeXt scaling (Hogan), seizure-specific intracranial pretraining (PPi), foundation clip models (CBraMod), and graph/transformer fusion (MGF-Net). | These are promising directions at different maturity levels and on different tasks. None can be ranked as a universal continuous detector from current evidence. |

Confidence is moderate for category selection and high for the narrow SzCORE challenge metrics and endpoint caveats. The challenge is non-peer-reviewed and its test set is private. Open-source status and architecture labels alone do not establish reliable clinical or edge deployment.
