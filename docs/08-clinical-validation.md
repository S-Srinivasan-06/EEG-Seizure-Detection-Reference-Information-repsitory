# 8. Clinical validation by setting and endpoint

[Contents](../README.md) | [Chapter 7: Methods and pretraining](07-methods-and-pretraining.md) | [Next: Agreement and interpretation](09-agreement-and-interpretation.md)

## What counts as clinical validation?

Different studies measure different outcomes. “Sensitivity” can mean the fraction of seizures detected, records containing at least one seizure, seizure-hours recognized, or patients correctly classified. These units are not interchangeable. A model flag is also separate from a staff action, treatment change, or patient outcome.

Beniczky and Ryvlin’s clinical-validation standards assess subjects, recordings, analysis and alarms, and the reference standard, organizing evaluation into phases 0-4 [V] ([original standards](https://doi.org/10.1111/epi.14049); [PMID 29873827](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29873827/)). This provides a clinical-validation axis alongside the research scoring and data conventions in SzCORE. Prospective data collection with retrospective offline analysis does not have the same evidential role as a live alarm intervention; a study phase should not be inferred from its title. The reviewed studies were not all classified against every phase criterion.

## Scalp epilepsy-monitoring units

Prospective device-performance evidence exists. Fürbass et al. evaluated EpiScan in three epilepsy-monitoring units (EMUs) and 205 patients. Sensitivity for unequivocal electrographic seizures was 81% (95% confidence interval [CI] 74-86%), with 7.1 false alarms per day [V] ([primary article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinph.2014.09.023)). This supports performance in a defined prospective setting, not randomized evidence of improved safety or outcomes.

Rommens et al. retrospectively compared staff recognition with Encevis and BESA in 115 people with 188 seizures and a separate 617-hour false-alarm sample [V]. Staff, Encevis, and BESA detected 67%, 67%, and 65% of seizures. Among 62 staff-missed events, Encevis detected 66% and BESA 39%. Median detection latencies were 31 seconds for staff, 10 seconds for Encevis, and 14 seconds for BESA; false-alarm rates were 4.9 and 2.1 per 24 hours [V] ([Rommens et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.yebeh.2018.04.026)). Despite “online” in the title, the algorithms were applied retrospectively and staff did not receive their alarms. The study identifies a possible earlier-signal opportunity, not observed gains in staff response, review time, or patient outcome.

Reus et al. analyzed 286 prolonged EEG records, including 249 seizures in 64 records. Record-level sensitivity, meaning detection of at least one seizure in a record, was 98% for Persyst, 98% for Encevis, and 95% for BESA. Seizure-level sensitivity, meaning recognition of all seizures in a record, was 93%, 88%, and 84%. Clinical physiologists scored 100% and 98% on those respective measures. Median false positives per record per 24 hours were 1.7, 5.5, and 2.4 [V] ([Reus et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.seizure.2022.01.009)). The comparison included live EMU observation, and the reference incorporated true software detections that physiologists had missed earlier. These are not blinded standalone-AI estimates.

Other retrospective commercial comparisons show operating-point trade-offs rather than a universal winner. Koren et al. (2021) report per-patient detection rates of 67.6-81.0%, 0.2-0.9 false alarms per hour, and 20-30-second delays in 81 patients [V]; a corrigendum corrected counts for two patients ([original paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/epi.16812); [corrigendum](https://doi.org/10.1111/epi.17527)). Their updated 2025 study included 80 patients, 6,826 hours, and 912 seizures. Across settings, Encevis 2.0 detected 80.7-89.1% with 12-22 false positives per day; Persyst 14 detected 67.3-71.1% with 2-4 per day [V] ([Koren et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinph.2025.04.003)). This retrospective evaluation included manufacturer-employed authors and did not measure staffing savings or outcomes.

## Adult inpatient and intensive care EEG

Ganguly et al. assessed Persyst retrospectively in a single health system, excluding EMU recordings. In a pilot, event-level generalized estimating equation (GEE) estimates were sensitivity 0.50 and positive predictive value (PPV) 0.60 against human detections. In the expanded cohort of 7,924 records from 2,854 people, record-level sensitivity was 0.91 (95% CI 0.88-0.93) and record-level PPV was 0.08 (0.07-0.09) [V] ([primary article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9162163/)). The expanded reference lacked precise seizure timing, so PPV 0.08 is not event-alarm precision. It asks whether a record containing at least one seizure was identified and cannot be compared with precision for individual alarms.

The CERTA randomized trial compared continuous EEG with routine EEG in adults with impaired consciousness and no recent seizure. Continuous monitoring detected more seizures and led to more treatment changes, without demonstrating a mortality benefit in the enrolled cohort [V] ([Rossetti et al.](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamaneurol.2020.2264)). CERTA is not an AI trial. It illustrates that detection, treatment changes, and patient outcomes are separate links in the care pathway.

## Neonatal EEG

The strongest randomized algorithm-assistance evidence in this review is ANSeR. Among 258 analyzable term neonates, individual-neonate seizure-recognition sensitivity was 81.3% with algorithm support versus 89.5% without it; specificity was 84.4% versus 89.1%. At the seizure-hour level, recognition was 66.0% (177/268) versus 45.3% (177/391), a difference of 20.8 percentage points (95% CI 3.6-37.1). Inappropriate antiseizure medication use did not differ significantly (37.5% versus 31.6%) [V] ([Pavel et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2352-4642(20)30239-X)). The seizure-hour benefit and individual-neonate results must remain distinct. The trial did not show improved long-term outcomes or reduced inappropriate medication, and neonatal performance should not be pooled with adult scalp EEG.

Hogan et al. (2025) report a major retrospective model-validation study ([published paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-024-01416-x); [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11711471/); PMID 39779830, PMCID PMC11711471). Their 21M-parameter ConvNeXt XL was trained on 202 development neonates and 50,299 summed per-channel EEG hours, not clock-hours, including 12,402 channel-level seizures. It was tested on held-out multi-reviewer Cork (n=51, same institution with no reviewer overlap) and external Helsinki (n=79) cohorts. Inputs were 16-second windows stepped every 0.25 seconds, smoothed over 32 seconds, thresholded at 0.5, with short-run cleanup.

Table 2 reports concatenated time-sample binary sensitivity, not event sensitivity: sensitivity/specificity were 88.9%/99.2% in Cork and 72.9%/98.5% in Helsinki; false event detections were 0.363/hour and 0.459/hour [V]. Table 3’s seizure-only Helsinki subset reports AUC 0.996 (interquartile range [IQR] 0.975-1.000), κ=.800, and 0.34 false detections/hour. For expert-replacement agreement, the difference-in-κ CIs span zero: Cork −.094 (−.189 to .005), p=.063; Helsinki −.082 (−.156 to .002), p=.055. This is no detected difference, not a formal noninferiority finding. No onset-latency result or CI for sensitivity/false detections was found. The validation is retrospective, bedside utility remains untested, and model-code availability was not found in the inspected source. The paper describes an open-source metrics framework.

## External adult inference and reader assistance

Yang et al. provide evidence against a blanket claim that external adult testing or review assistance is absent. A TUH-trained ConvLSTM was run in inference-only mode on 1,006 Australian RPAH sessions, nearly 14,590 hours. The journal abstract reports sensitivity of 76.68% and nearly 56 false alarms per 24 hours [V]. This is a large external institutional scalp EEG inference test on historical records with legacy expert annotations, and it has substantial alarm burden. The abstract’s `[0,115]` false-alarm summary is ambiguous as to statistic; do not call it a range or interquartile range without full-text confirmation.

A separate 66-session human-assisted pilot reported 92.19% seizure recognition and review time of 7.62 versus 90 minutes per 24-hour, 18-channel EEG [V] ([Yang et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eswa.2022.118083)). These pilot figures do not describe the full 1,006-session external cohort. This was not a randomized live-service intervention. “Prospective inference” does not mean prospective patient enrollment or model updating. Full journal methods and confidence intervals were not accessible in the audit, so that access limitation remains.

## Ambulatory, wearable, and subscalp EEG

Hartmann et al. evaluated two-channel derivations from scalp EEG for prolonged mobile/ambulatory review, not actual implanted subscalp recordings. In 48 independent-center patients, the study reports mean sensitivity of 88.8% and 12.9 false positives per day [V] ([Hartmann et al.](https://doi.org/10.1111/epi.17259)). A 2025 CNN-BiLSTM study on actual subscalp recordings from 16 patients was retrospective. Its cross-modal scalp-plus-subscalp setup reports 94% sensitivity and 1.11 false detections per day; an individual first-half subscalp training/testing setup reports 87% and 5.9 per day [V] (PMID [41056137](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41056137/)). These results are specific to small cohorts and protocols; no prospective workload or outcome benefit is established.

The 2026 SeizeIT2 analysis collected data prospectively at multiple centers but evaluated the algorithm offline with blinded human review; it was not live alarm deployment. Among 192 adults and 616 focal seizures, algorithm-alone sensitivity was 0.73 and precision 0.004. After expert review, sensitivity was 0.31 and precision 0.83 [V] ([Swinnen et al.](https://doi.org/10.1002/epi4.70260)). The change shows the candidate-review trade-off. A post-hoc sensitivity of 0.74 for seizures with both a clear behind-ear pattern and ictal tachycardia is a selected multimodal subgroup, not general wearable performance.

UMPIRE prospectively studied bilateral subscalp acquisition in 26 adults. Manual review found clinically useful findings in 23/26. However, complete recordings were not independently reviewed outside periods flagged by scalp algorithms or diaries, so detector sensitivity, specificity, and false detections cannot be estimated [V] ([Halliday et al.](https://doi.org/10.1111/epi.18458)). This is acquisition and feasibility evidence, not detector validation. Responsive neurostimulation (RNS) is likewise a patient-specific closed-loop intracranial detection/stimulation system; its FDA feasibility evidence is not a general-purpose seizure alarm sensitivity/specificity study ([FDA summary of safety and effectiveness](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf10/P100026B.pdf)).

## Intracranial EEG and scalp visibility

PPi and long-term intracranial EEG (iEEG) studies address SEEG or implanted electrocorticography (ECoG) and should remain modality-specific. Sun et al. report continuous SWEC-ETHZ and TJU-HH event results in the primary abstract: sensitivity 97.5%/98.1%, false detections 0.06/0.22 per hour, and delays of 13.7/9.9 seconds [V] ([Sun et al.](https://doi.org/10.1109/JBHI.2022.3199206)). Split independence was not verified in the accessible abstract. These are not scalp-detector metrics.

Simultaneous scalp/SEEG recordings clarify a separate visibility limit. Among 172 SEEG seizures in 27 surgical patients, 100 had scalp ictal patterns; scalp visibility was 33% for focal-aware and 33% for subclinical seizures [V] ([Casale et al.](https://doi.org/10.1097/WNP.0000000000000739)). This selected cohort shows that some intracranially confirmed events were not visible on scalp EEG. It does not set a universal scalp sensitivity ceiling or prove all scalp-negative events are machine-undetectable.

## Regulatory status and intended use

The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) database records a 510(k) substantially-equivalent decision for Ceribell Infant Seizure Detection Software, K252070, dated 21 November 2025 [V]. Its labeling concerns marking previously acquired EEG for qualified practitioners, including neonates of 25-44 weeks postmenstrual age and infants younger than one year [V] ([FDA decision record](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfpmn/pmn.cfm?ID=K252070); [official summary and labeling](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf25/K252070.pdf)). This is a jurisdiction- and intended-use-specific clearance. Neither the decision nor a benchmark establishes improved outcomes. These official records were rechecked on 30 September 2026; an exhaustive global device/version clearance census was not completed.

## Evidence boundary

The studies above include prospective device performance, retrospective external inference, a randomized neonatal assistance trial, and small review-workflow studies. The overall evidence for routine adult staffing reduction, improved adult patient outcomes, and broadly transportable alarm performance remains limited in this targeted review. Confidence is high for the stated endpoint distinctions and cohort results based on primary reports; confidence is moderate for this overall limitation because the dossier is not an exhaustive systematic review.
